% ---------- INTRODUCTION ----------
\section{Introduction}

People increasingly supervise rather than directly operate automated systems, from aviation flight decks to medical monitoring and driver-assistance aids. In these settings the human is expected to step in when the automation reaches the edge of its competence, which presupposes that the operator understands what the system is doing and why. To support this, systems are designed to be \textit{transparent} and to \textit{explain} their behaviour. A growing body of work in explainable artificial intelligence (XAI), however, reports a worrying dissociation: explanations can raise users' confidence and feeling of being informed without improving their ability to detect when the system is wrong \parencite{Bansal2021,WangYin2021}.

A subtle but important point is often left implicit in this literature. Producing an explanation is not the same as the explanation being understood and acted upon. We refer to the former as \textit{explainability} and to the latter as \textit{explicability}. Most studies that manipulate transparency vary \textit{how much} information the system communicates \parencite{Mercado2016,Stowers2017}, conflating the \textit{content} of an explanation with its \textit{form} (its wording, framing, and presentation). It therefore remains unclear whether the form of an explanation, on its own, shapes how well an operator's mental model is updated, particularly when attention is divided across competing tasks.

This report investigates that question. Holding the action-relevant information constant, we compare three forms of explanation delivered by an automation aid in a multitasking environment and measure both a subjective response (perceived transparency) and an objective indicator of whether the operator's mental model was actually updated. The remainder of the report is structured as follows. Section~2 reviews the relevant lecture content and literature and defines our central terms. Section~3 states the research gap, question, and hypotheses. Section~4 describes the study design, materials, procedure, and measures. Sections~5--7 report the results, discuss them in relation to prior work, and conclude.

% ---------- BACKGROUND ----------
\section{Background}

\subsection{Definitions of terms}
We distinguish three related but separate constructs that recur throughout the lecture and the literature.

\textit{Transparency} is the degree to which a system provides the operator with insight into its inner workings, intentions, and reasoning process. Frameworks such as the Situation Awareness-based Agent Transparency (SAT) model describe transparency in terms of \textit{levels} of information the agent communicates --- what it is doing, why, and what it predicts \parencite{Chen2014,Mercado2016}.

\textit{Explainability} is the system's act of producing an explanation of what it did and how. It is a property of the system's output.

\textit{Explicability} is whether that explanation is presented in a way that it is actually understood and processed by the operator. It is a property of the human--system interaction, not of the output alone. Two explanations can be equally explainable (containing the same facts) yet differ in explicability.

\textit{Trustworthiness} is a broad concept; in this study we focus on the operator's \textit{subjective trust in the automation aid} --- the felt confidence and dependability that drives reliance. Because transparency carries the subjective side of our main hypothesis, we measure trust only once, as a forced-choice preference at the end of the session, drawing on the trust-in-automation tradition \parencite{Jian2000}.

\subsection{Relation to the lecture}
The project is anchored in two lecture topics. The \textit{transparency} block addresses the question of what level of information an aid should communicate to the operator. The \textit{explainability and explicability} block introduces criteria for good explanations --- among them \textit{selectivity} (an explanation should pick out the relevant causes rather than report everything), \textit{contrastiveness} (explaining why \textit{this} happened rather than some expected alternative), and \textit{actionability} (telling the operator what to do). These criteria, drawn from the social-science account of explanation \parencite{Miller2019}, motivate our design. Crucially, we deliberately do \textit{not} manipulate selectivity: selecting fewer facts removes information and would re-introduce the very amount-of-information confound the research gap targets. We therefore hold the information content constant and isolate \textit{contrastiveness} (how the same facts are framed) and \textit{actionability} (an added operator cue).

\subsection{Prior empirical work}
Transparency studies in human--agent teaming show that giving operators more information about an agent's reasoning and uncertainty can improve trust calibration and decision quality \parencite{Mercado2016,Stowers2017}. These studies vary the \textit{level} of information but do not separate information \textit{content} from \textit{presentation}. In parallel, XAI research on AI-assisted decision-making finds that explanations frequently increase users' agreement and subjective sense of understanding without a matching increase in objective understanding or error detection \parencite{Bansal2021,WangYin2021}. Crucially, these dissociation findings come almost entirely from single-task settings, where the user can devote full attention to the explanation.

\subsection{Research gap}
Two gaps follow. First, prior transparency work confounds the content of an explanation with its form; it is therefore not known whether form alone --- holding action-relevant content constant --- affects perceived transparency and the operator's mental model. Second, the explainability--explicability dissociation has been demonstrated under full attention but not under \textit{multitasking load}, the very condition under which supervisory operators usually work. If a long, status-log-style explanation makes operators feel informed without enabling them to anticipate the system's next action, they may miss automation errors with far-reaching consequences in domains such as aviation, medical monitoring, and autonomous driving aids.

% ---------- OWN FOCUS ----------
\section{Research Question and Hypotheses}

\textbf{Research questions.} Both ask whether the \textit{linguistic form} of an automation aid's reasoning explanation --- holding information content constant under multitasking load --- matters, but they target the two halves of the explainability/explicability distinction:

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 (subjective).] Does the form of the explanation change the operator's \textit{subjective transparency} --- the feeling of being informed? \textit{(addressed by H1)}
  \item[RQ2 (objective).] Does the form of the explanation change whether the operator's \textit{mental model actually updates} --- objective explicability? \textit{(addressed by H2)}
\end{description}

We compare three forms of explanation that all communicate the same action-relevant facts (see Section~\ref{sec:forms}). The comparison is therefore between \textit{forms} of explanation, not between explanation and no explanation. Hypotheses H1 and H2 are the load-bearing pair: their opposite predicted directions are the operational signature of explainability~$\neq$~explicability. H3 and H4 are supporting, and a workload item provides a mechanism check.

\begin{description}
  \item[H1 (verbose feels more transparent).] Subjective transparency is highest under the verbose form (F1), even though the information content is identical across forms.
  \item[H2 (contrastive form improves explicability).] Post-block mental-model accuracy is highest under the contrastive forms (F2/F3), even though the information content is identical across forms.
  \item[H3 (contrastive framing aids extraction; supporting).] Because the action-relevant fact is easier to read off a contrastive panel, participants detect a higher proportion of automation-missed events under F2/F3 than under F1.
  \item[H4 (saved reading time is redeployed; supporting).] Participants achieve higher performance on the non-aided tasks (communications, resource management, and scheduling) under F2/F3 than under F1.
  \item[Mechanism check (workload).] Subjective workload is higher under F1. This tests whether any F1 transparency advantage is confounded by feeling overwhelmed, and supplies the mediating step behind H2 and H4 (verbose~$\rightarrow$~higher load~$\rightarrow$~worse extraction and less spare capacity).
\end{description}

The reasoning behind H1 is that a continuous, detailed stream of information makes the system \textit{feel} maximally informative. H2--H4 follow from the prediction that contrastive framing and an actionable cue make the same information easier to process under load, freeing attention and directing it to the events that matter.

% ---------- METHOD ----------
\section{Method}

\subsection{Participants}
We plan to recruit approximately twenty participants from the experimenters' personal environment (fellow students, friends, family), consistent with the scope of this pilot study. Because we use a within-subjects design, every participant contributes to all three conditions. Demographic data (age, gender) are collected and reported. As this is a small pilot rather than a study intended for population-level conclusions, no ethical approval was required. \textit{[Final sample size and demographics to be reported after data collection.]}

\subsection{Materials and apparatus}
The study runs on \textbf{OpenMATB} \parencite{Cegarra2020}, an open-source reimplementation of NASA's Multi-Attribute Task Battery \parencite{Comstock1992}. OpenMATB presents several supervisory subtasks concurrently; participants actively perform all four of them: \textit{system monitoring} (watching gauges and warning lights and responding to out-of-range states), \textit{communications}, \textit{resource management} (pump and tank management), and \textit{scheduling}. System monitoring is the only subtask supported by an automation aid; the other three compete for attention and are never aided.

The automation aid operates on the system-monitoring task at approximately 78\% reliability. Each block contains \textbf{nine events}, one every 30\,s: five \textit{routine} events the aid handles in time, two \textit{near-misses} the aid handles just in time, and two \textit{misses} on which the aid does not act, so that --- if the information is perceived and processed --- the operator can still handle the event manually (7 of 9 handled appropriately $\approx$ 78\%). The aid's behaviour, reliability, and failure timeline are identical across conditions; only the \textit{form} of the explanation panel changes.

\subsubsection{The three forms of explanation}
\label{sec:forms}
A panel fires on \textit{every} event in \textit{every} form (nine times per block), reporting the same action-relevant facts: which indicator, whether it was in or out of range, whether the aid acted, and (for near-misses) the time-to-failure. The three forms differ only in their wording (Table~\ref{tab:forms}). The verbose form adds grammatical scaffolding and redundant exact sensor values but introduces no new action-relevant fact. This content matching is the core of the design: the information an operator needs to catch an automation failure is equally present in F1, F2, and F3, so the F1$\leftrightarrow$F2 contrast isolates \textit{contrastiveness} and the F2$\leftrightarrow$F3 contrast isolates \textit{actionability}.

\subsection{Design}
The study uses a within-subjects design. Each participant completes all three forms (F1/F2/F3), each paired with one of three different gauge sets (A, B, C). The gauge sets contain different subsets of gauges and lights requiring attention, so that the post-block memory probe cannot be answered from another block's content. The order of F1/F2/F3 is counterbalanced across participants with a Latin square to mitigate learning and order effects; because blocks are crossed with forms, every form meets every gauge set equally often, with no confound between condition and the probe's correct answers.

\subsection{Procedure}
Each session lasts approximately 20 minutes: a 3-minute practice phase, followed by three 5-minute experimental blocks (one per form), followed by a debriefing. A lean post-block battery (about nine items) is administered after each block in a fixed order --- the objective mental-model probe \textit{first} (to capture the mental model before reflection contaminates it), then subjective transparency, then the single workload item \textit{last} (so that rating effort does not colour the earlier answers). The battery pauses the MATB clock and is kept short so that it does not itself become a load manipulation between blocks. The platform logs behaviour continuously, and one final preference questionnaire is completed at the end.

\subsubsection{Briefing and debriefing}
During the \textit{briefing}, participants are told in clear terms that they will interact with different AI-supported versions of a monitoring system and then answer questions about their experience, that participation is voluntary and can be stopped at any time, and that responses are confidential and used only for research. To avoid biasing behaviour, the concrete research question, the hypotheses, and our expectations about the outcome are \textit{not} disclosed beforehand. The true purpose of the study --- the comparison of explanation forms and the explainability/explicability dissociation --- is explained afterwards in the \textit{debriefing}.

\subsection{Measures and operationalisation}
Each measure maps onto a specific hypothesis.

\textit{Mental-model probe (H2; objective).} After each block the participant answers five sliders implemented with OpenMATB's \texttt{genericscales}, which allow no number entry --- each answer is a position between two verbal anchors. Three \textit{calibration} sliders ask how often the aid failed to step in, how often it acted only just in time, and how reliable it was overall; two \textit{recognition} sliders ask whether the aid acted on a named indicator during the block. Responses are scored offline against the block's ground truth (misses $=2$, near-misses $=2$, reliability $\approx 78\%$; recognition keyed to whether the probed indicator was in the block's set) to yield an objective explicability score. The recognition answers are balanced across blocks (Yes/Yes, Yes/No, No/No), which also defeats an ``the aid always acts'' response bias: a participant who is not tracking cannot score well by answering uniformly. Slider defaults are neutral, so they do not anchor the answer.

\textit{Subjective transparency (H1).} Three sliders (strongly disagree--strongly agree) capture the feeling of being informed: that the system told the operator what it was doing, that the operator could easily keep track of what the automation was doing, and that the amount of information felt about right (an item on which both too little and too much are unfavourable). At least one item is reverse-scored to detect straight-lining at $N\approx 20$. We deliberately use researcher-designed items rather than a validated instrument: prior transparency studies manipulated transparency as an independent variable and did not measure it subjectively \parencite{Mercado2016,Stowers2017}, comparable XAI work relied on study-specific subjective-understanding items \parencite{WangYin2021}, and published explanation-satisfaction scales were built for a discrete explanation attached to a one-shot decision and contain detail-monotone items that would let the verbose form (F1) score highest by construction, biasing H1 before data collection. Paired with the probe, these items test the H1 dissociation between feeling informed and being accurate.

\textit{Workload (mechanism check).} A single Paas-style mental-effort slider (very low--very high) asks how mentally demanding the block felt. Within-subjects, we need only the direction (is F1 higher load?), not the full NASA-TLX decomposition; the item is declared a priori as a mediation check, which keeps the post-block battery short.

\textit{Detection of automation misses (H3; log).} Whenever the aid skips an event the operator should catch it manually; we log hit/miss and reaction time, pooling the six misses per participant (two per block) to stabilise the estimate.

\textit{Non-aided task performance (H4; log).} Communications, resource-management, and scheduling performance are logged to test whether reading less in the explanation panel frees attention for the unaided tasks.

\textit{Preference debrief (trust; triangulation).} Five forced-choice items at the end of the session ask which form felt most informative, most useful, most trustworthy, least distracting, and would be self-chosen for a future task. This is the only place trust is measured; firing once, it adds negligible load while triangulating the objective measures.

\subsection{Planned analysis}
Given the small expected sample, analysis will emphasise descriptive statistics (means, medians, standard deviations) and visual evaluation (e.g.\ boxplots and bar charts with error bars comparing F1/F2/F3, and scatter plots relating subjective transparency to objective mental-model accuracy). For each dependent variable we plan a within-subjects omnibus across the three forms (repeated-measures ANOVA, or Friedman if assumptions fail) with assumption tests stated explicitly, followed by two planned contrasts: F1 vs.\ \{F2,\,F3\} for the contrastiveness effect and F2 vs.\ F3 for the actionability effect. The core explainability~$\neq$~explicability test is the \textit{form}~$\times$~\textit{measure-type} interaction (subjective transparency vs.\ objective accuracy, z-scored to a common scale): a significant interaction in opposite directions is the crossover. At $N\approx 20$ we prefer mixed-effects models (random intercepts per participant and per gauge set, block order as a covariate) and report effect sizes with confidence intervals throughout. H1 and H2 are pre-registered as confirmatory; H3 and H4, with few miss trials per block, are treated as exploratory. \textit{[Final analysis and tooling to be reported with the results.]}

\textit{Known limitations to be discussed.} Holding content constant makes the manipulation deliberately \textit{subtle} (only wording differs), so effects may be small at $N\approx 20$; this is an honest trade of \textit{novel-but-subtle} against \textit{strong-but-unoriginal}, since a frequency or fact-count change would re-introduce the amount-of-information confound and close the research gap. The within-subjects design and the F1$\leftrightarrow$F2 / F2$\leftrightarrow$F3 contrasts let us attribute any effect to contrastiveness and actionability separately. Load is held at a single moderate level, so generalisation across load levels is not possible. The number of automation misses per block is small (two per five-minute block), and the sample is far below the 50--100 participants typical in this literature, so statistical power for H3/H4 in particular is limited.

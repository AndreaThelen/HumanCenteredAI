% ---------- INTRODUCTION ----------
\section{Introduction}

People increasingly supervise rather than directly operate automated systems, from aviation flight decks to medical monitoring and driver-assistance aids. In these settings the human is expected to step in when the automation reaches its limits, which only works if the operator understands what the system is doing. The standard procedure to achieve this is to make systems \textit{transparent} and to have them \textit{explain} their behaviour. Yet producing an explanation is not the same as the explanation being understood and acted upon --- a distinction we sharpen in Section~2 as \textit{explainability} versus \textit{explicability}. Indeed, work in explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) reports a worrying dissociation: explanations can raise users' confidence and feeling of being informed without improving their ability to detect when the system is wrong \parencite{Bansal2021,WangYin2021}.

This report asks whether the \textit{linguistic form} of an explanation --- its wording, framing, and presentation --- changes that dissociation when the information content is held constant and attention is divided across competing tasks. We compare three forms of explanation delivered by an automation aid in a multitasking environment and measure both a subjective response (perceived transparency) and an objective indicator of whether the operator's mental model was actually updated. The remainder of the report is structured as follows. Section~2 reviews the relevant lecture content and literature and defines our central terms. Section~3 states the research gap, question, and hypotheses. Section~4 describes the study design, materials, procedure, and measures. Sections~5--7 report the results, discuss them in relation to prior work, and conclude.

% ---------- BACKGROUND ----------
\section{Background}

\subsection{Definitions of terms}
We distinguish three related but separate constructs that recur throughout the lecture and the literature.

\textit{Transparency} is the degree to which a system provides the operator with insight into its inner workings, intentions, and reasoning process. Frameworks such as the Situation Awareness-based Agent Transparency (SAT) model describe transparency in terms of \textit{levels} of information the agent communicates --- what it is doing, why, and what it predicts \parencite{Chen2014,Mercado2016}.

\textit{Explainability} is the system's act of producing an explanation of what it did and how. It is a property of the system's output.

\textit{Explicability} is whether that explanation is presented in a way that it is actually understood and processed by the operator. It is a property of the human--system interaction, not of the output alone. Two explanations can be equally explainable (containing the same facts) yet differ in explicability.

\subsection{Relation to the lecture}
The project is anchored in two lecture topics. The \textit{transparency} block addresses the question of what level of information an aid should communicate to the operator. The \textit{explainability and explicability} block introduces criteria for good explanations --- among them \textit{contrastiveness} and \textit{actionability}. These criteria, drawn from the social-science account of explanation \parencite{Miller2019}, motivate our design. Crucially, we deliberately do \textit{not} manipulate selectivity: selecting fewer facts removes information and would be in line with existing research and would not support the research gap we want to target. We therefore hold the information content constant and isolate \textit{contrastiveness} (how the same facts are framed) and \textit{actionability} (an added operator cue).

\subsection{Prior empirical work}
Transparency studies in human--agent teaming show that giving operators more information about an agent's reasoning and uncertainty can improve trust calibration and decision quality \parencite{Mercado2016,Stowers2017}. These studies vary the \textit{level} of information but do not separate information \textit{content} from \textit{presentation}. In parallel, XAI research on AI-assisted decision-making finds that explanations frequently increase users' agreement and subjective sense of understanding without a matching increase in objective understanding or error detection \parencite{Bansal2021,WangYin2021}. Crucially, these dissociation findings come almost entirely from single-task settings, where the user can devote full attention to the explanation.

\subsection{Research gap}
Two gaps follow. First, prior transparency work does not distinguish the content of an explanation with its linguistic form; it is therefore not known whether linguistic form alone --- holding information content constant --- affects perceived transparency and the operator's mental model. Second, the explainability--explicability dissociation has been demonstrated under full attention but not under \textit{multitasking load}, the very condition under which supervisory operators usually work. If a long, status-log-style explanation makes operators feel informed without enabling them to anticipate the system's next action, they may miss automation errors with far-reaching consequences in domains such as aviation, medical monitoring, and autonomous driving aids.

% ---------- OWN FOCUS ----------
\section{Research Question and Hypotheses}

\textbf{Research questions.} Both ask whether the \textit{linguistic form} of an automation aid's reasoning explanation --- holding information content constant under multitasking load --- matters, but they target the two different aspects of the explainability--explicability dissociation: the subjective feeling of being informed and the objective mental-model update.

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 (subjective).] Does the form of the explanation change the operator's \textit{subjective transparency} --- the feeling of being informed? \textit{(addressed by H1)}
  \item[RQ2 (objective).] Does the form of the explanation change whether the operator's \textit{mental model actually updates} --- objective explicability? \textit{(addressed by H2)}
\end{description}

We compare three forms of explanation that all communicate the same facts (see Section~\ref{sec:forms}). The comparison is therefore between \textit{linguistic forms} of explanation, not between the amount of information provided. Hypotheses H1 and H2 are the load-bearing pair: The fact that they predict opposite directions is a key sign that explainability and explicability are not the same. H3 and H4 are supporting hypotheses.

\begin{description}
  \item[H1 (verbose feels more transparent).] Subjective transparency is highest under the verbose form (F1), even though the information content is identical across forms.
  \item[H2 (contrastive form improves explicability).] Post-block mental-model accuracy is highest under the contrastive forms (F2/F3), even though the information content is identical across forms.
  \item[H3 (contrastive form improves miss detection).] Because the action-relevant fact is easier to read off a contrastive panel, participants detect a higher proportion of automation-missed events under F2/F3 than under F1.
  \item[H4 (saved reading time is redeployed).] Participants achieve higher performance on the non-aided tasks (communications and resource management) under F2/F3 than under F1.
\end{description}

The reasoning behind H1 is that a detailed stream of information makes the system \textit{feel} maximally informative. H2--H4 follow from the prediction that contrastive framing and an actionable cue make the same information easier to process under load, freeing attention and directing it to the events that matter.

% ---------- REFERENCE TO OHTER GROUPS ----------
\subsection{Reference to other groups}
Most groups built on the lecture constructs: transparency, trust, mental models, workload. The closest to us vary the \textit{wording} of the aid's messages: \textbf{AH~Group} (actionable vs.\ generic), \textbf{Group~P} (concrete vs.\ vague warnings), \textbf{Group~A} (calm vs.\ neutral tone), team \textbf{It-takes-two} (reason-giving vs.\ status-only), and especially \textbf{Group~K}, which compares no/vague/clear failure messages with length matching and a comprehension check. Other groups are more distant: they add an explanation rather than reframing it, manipulate the system itself (reliability, automation level, or task load) rather than its message, or treat trust as a personal trait or social signal.

What is unique to us is that we hold the \textit{information content constant} across all three forms, isolating linguistic form rather than ``more vs.\ less'' explanation; and we separate \textit{explainability} from \textit{explicability}, predicting them to move in opposite directions (the verbose form feels most transparent, H1, yet the contrastive forms produce the better-updated mental model, H2). Where the majority of our collegues asks ``does clearer explanation raise trust?'', we ask whether an explanation can raise the \textit{feeling} of being informed without improving what the operator actually \textit{knows} --- captured by an objective mental-model probe that, across all groups, only we use.

\subsection{Materials}
The study runs on \textbf{OpenMATB} \parencite{Cegarra2020}, an open-source reimplementation of NASA's Multi-Attribute Task Battery \parencite{Comstock1992}. OpenMATB presents several supervisory subtasks concurrently; participants actively perform all three of them: \textit{system monitoring} (watching gauges and warning lights and responding to out-of-range states), \textit{communications}, and \textit{resource management} (pump and tank management). System monitoring is the only subtask supported by an automation aid; the other two compete for attention and are never aided.

The automation aid operates on the system-monitoring task at approximately 78\% reliability. Each block contains \textbf{nine events}, one every 30\,s: five \textit{routine} events the aid handles in time, two \textit{near-misses} the aid handles just in time, and two \textit{misses} on which the aid does not act, so that --- if the information is perceived and processed --- the operator can still handle the event manually (7 of 9 handled appropriately $\approx$ 78\%). The aid's behaviour, reliability, and failure timeline are identical across conditions; only the \textit{linguistic form} of the explanation panel changes.

\subsubsection{The three forms of explanation}
\label{sec:forms}
A panel fires on \textit{every} event in \textit{every} form (nine times per block), reporting the same facts: which indicator, whether it was in or out of range, whether the aid acted, and (for near-misses) the time-to-failure. The three forms differ only in their wording (Table~\ref{tab:forms}). The verbose form adds grammatical scaffolding and redundant exact sensor values but introduces no new content with respect to catching a failure. This content matching is the core of the design: the information an operator needs to catch an automation failure is equally present in F1, F2, and F3, so the F1$\leftrightarrow$F2 contrast isolates \textit{contrastiveness} and the F2$\leftrightarrow$F3 contrast isolates \textit{actionability}.

\subsection{Design}
The study uses a within-subjects design. Each participant completes all three forms (F1/F2/F3), each paired with one of three different gauge sets (A, B, C). The gauge sets contain different subsets of gauges and lights requiring attention, so that the post-block memory probe cannot be answered from another block's content. The order of F1/F2/F3 is counterbalanced across participants with a Latin square to mitigate learning and order effects; because blocks are crossed with forms, every form meets every gauge set equally often.

\subsection{Procedure}
Each session lasts approximately 20 minutes: a 3-minute practice phase, followed by three 5-minute experimental blocks (one per form), followed by a debriefing. After each block questionnaires are used in a fixed order --- the objective mental-model probe \textit{first} (to capture the mental model before reflection contaminates it), then subjective transparency, then the single workload item \textit{last}. One final preference questionnaire is completed at the end.

\subsection{Measures}
Each measure maps onto one hypothesis. The questionnaire items are OpenMATB \texttt{genericscales} sliders presented after each block in the fixed order; every slider starts at a neutral position. The two behavioural measures (H3, H4) are read from the continuous platform log.

\subsubsection{H1 --- Subjective transparency}
Three sliders capture the \textit{feeling} of being informed (anchors in brackets):
\begin{itemize}
  \item ``The system told me what it was doing.'' \textit{(strongly disagree--strongly agree)}
  \item ``It was hard to keep track of what the automation was doing.'' \textit{(strongly disagree--strongly agree, reverse-scored)}
  \item ``The amount of information the system gave me felt about right.'' \textit{(strongly disagree--strongly agree)}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{H2 --- Mental-model probe}
Five sliders measure whether the operator's mental model actually updated. Three \textit{calibration} items rate the aid's error profile (anchors 
 in brackets):
\begin{itemize}
  \item ``How often did the automation fail to act on an indicator that needed attention?'' \textit{(never--on most events)}
  \item ``How often did the automation step in only just in time --- a close call?'' \textit{(never--on most events)}
  \item ``Overall, how reliable was the automation this block?'' \textit{(not at all--completely reliable)}
\end{itemize}
Two \textit{recognition} items ask whether the aid acted on a named indicator:
\begin{itemize}
  \item ``Did the automation act on Scale~X at any point during this block?'' \textit{(yes--no)}
  \item ``Did the automation act on Warning light~Y at any point during this block?'' \textit{(yes--no)}
\end{itemize}
The probed indicators differ by block and their correct answers are balanced (Yes/Yes, Yes/No, No/No) across the three blocks. The responses are scored offline against the block ground truth to create an objective explicability score.

\subsubsection{H3 --- Detection of automation misses}
Read from the platform log and count the number of automation-missed events (two per block) that the participant successfully detects and handles manually. The proportion of detected misses is compared across forms. For catched misses, calculate the reaction time from the moment the aid's panel fires to the moment the participant clicks on the relevant gauge or light.

\subsubsection{H4 --- Non-aided task performance}
\label{sec:h4-measures}
H4 tests whether the attention freed by an easier-to-read explanation panel is redeployed to the two tasks the aid never handles: resource management and communications. Both are read directly from the continuous OpenMATB platform log, so no questionnaire is involved. We report two measures per task, chosen to be the standard MATB readouts for that task \parencite{Comstock1992,SantiagoEspada2011,Cegarra2020}.

\paragraph{Resource management.} Two depleting tanks (A and B) must be held near a target level of 2500 units. We report (i) the \textbf{root-mean-square deviation (RMSD)} of the tank level from target, averaged over the two tanks (lower is better), and (ii) the \textbf{proportion of time the tanks stayed within tolerance} ($\pm 500$ units, i.e.\ the 2000--3000 band; higher is better). RMSD captures the typical size of the deviation the operator allowed to build up, while time-in-tolerance captures how much of the block was spent in an acceptable state; together they index how well the unaided regulation task was maintained \parencite{Comstock1992,SantiagoEspada2011}.

\paragraph{Communications.} The operator must respond to own-call-sign radio requests by tuning the named radio to the requested frequency, while ignoring distractor call signs. We report (i) \textbf{response accuracy}, the proportion of own-call-sign trials answered correctly (higher is better), and (ii) the \textbf{response time on hits}, the latency in milliseconds from the end of the call to a validated response (lower is better). Accuracy indexes how reliably the secondary auditory task was serviced, and response time indexes how promptly, which is the more sensitive marker of spare attentional capacity under load \parencite{Cegarra2020}.
